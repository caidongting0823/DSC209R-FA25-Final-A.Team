1. What have you done so far?

So far, we have built an explorable explanation called “History in Motion – 1989 Loma Prieta Earthquake” that focuses on the foreshocks, main shock, and aftershocks of the 1989 Loma Prieta event using the USGS earthquake catalog (the same `us_earthquakes_m4.5_cleaned.csv` dataset from Project 3, filtered to 1989–1990 in the conterminous US). Our current prototype uses a two-column scrollytelling layout: narrative “chapters” on the left (“Foreshocks,” “Main Shock,” “Aftershocks,” and “Rebuilding”) and a sticky map panel on the right that stays fixed while the user scrolls. The map panel shows a D3 Albers USA map with earthquakes encoded as circles whose size reflects magnitude and whose styling highlights the Loma Prieta main shock, along with tooltips that reveal place, magnitude, and time on hover. We have also implemented a time slider and play/pause controls that animate the sequence from April 1989 to April 1990, plus a mini-timeline area chart showing the daily count of earthquakes, with a moving cursor tied to the slider. Finally, we connected the narrative and the visualization: entering each chapter automatically moves the slider to a relevant point in time (e.g., foreshocks, main shock day, aftershock period), so the story text and the evolving seismic pattern stay in sync.

2. What will be the most challenging part of your project to design, and why?

The most challenging part will be designing the narrative and interactions so that they form a clear, compelling story rather than just a set of “cool” features. Based on the final project rubric, we need to follow a strong story structure (e.g., and–but–therefore), provide meaningful annotations, and end with a memorable takeaway, so we must carefully decide which moments in the Loma Prieta sequence to emphasize and how to visually highlight them. It will also be difficult to balance guided scrollytelling with open exploration: users should be able to play through the timeline, scrub to specific days, or jump via the chapters without getting lost or confused about “where” they are in the sequence. On the visualization side, we need to refine our encodings and interactions so that patterns in foreshocks and aftershocks are legible without overplotting, while still keeping the design clean and readable on a typical laptop screen. Finally, integrating additional context (such as damage, infrastructure failures, or policy changes) as overlays or annotations—without cluttering the map or overwhelming viewers with text—will require careful iterative design and testing.
